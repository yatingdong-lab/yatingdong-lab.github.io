Research

From plant adaptation to medicinal crop improvement

A single question runs through everything we do. How do plants reorganize themselves, their genomes, gene networks, and morphology, to meet the environments they live in? We approach this question with high-throughput omics, phenomics, and systems biology, across systems ranging from polyploid cotton and the liverwort Marchantia to the medicinal plants that define East Asian pharmacopoeia. The answers, we believe, are not only of evolutionary interest. They form the molecular foundation for breeding better and more sustainable medicinal crops.

01 · Theme

Genomic foundations of plant adaptation

Plants face two recurring evolutionary challenges. Their own genomes change through whole-genome duplication and polyploidization, and the environments they occupy change, sometimes radically, as in the conquest of land. Both events demand a wholesale reorganization of gene regulatory networks. Our long-standing interest is in understanding how that reorganization happens at the molecular level, and what general principles emerge.

In cotton (Gossypium), an allopolyploid crop whose two subgenomes were merged only one to two million years ago, we have characterized the striking diversity of salt tolerance across diploid and polyploid species, and dissected how duplicated genomes coordinate their transcriptomic response to salinity stress. By contrasting salt-sensitive and salt-tolerant species at both the phenotypic and regulatory levels, we have begun to define the gene expression patterns that underlie adaptive variation in this lineage. In the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha, a tractable representative of the earliest land plants, we have investigated how core nutrient-sensing pathways are deployed and diversified, offering a window into the regulatory innovations that accompanied plant terrestrialization.

Together, these systems form a comparative platform. From a young allopolyploid crop to an ancient bryophyte, we ask which features of plant adaptation are deeply conserved, which are lineage-specific innovations, and how this knowledge can be transferred to medicinal plants, themselves often polyploid and often grown across heterogeneous environments.

02 · Theme

Evolution and development of medicinal plant traits

Many medicinal plants owe their value to specialized organs such as rhizomes, tubers, glandular trichomes, and lignified roots, which synthesize and store the bioactive compounds humans have come to depend on. Yet for most of these species, we still do not know when these structures arose in evolution, how they develop, or which gene regulatory programs underwrite them. This gap is a striking contrast to the depth of knowledge available for model species and major staple crops.

We aim to close that gap, using Atractylodes macrocephala (白术) and its distinctive rhizome as a flagship system. The rhizome is both an organ of perennial survival and the principal site of medicinal compound accumulation, making it an unusually integrated case study in evo-devo. By combining high-quality genome assemblies, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, comparative genomics across related Asteraceae, and developmental analysis, we ask how this organ originated, how its cell-type diversity is patterned, and how its metabolic and morphological identities are coordinated.

More broadly, we view medicinal plants as an underexplored treasure of plant evolution. Their long history of human selection, their phylogenetic breadth, and the very specificity of the traits they produce make them ideal not just for applied breeding, but for fundamental questions about how plant form and chemistry evolve together.

03 · Theme

Sustainable production of medicinal crops

Medicinal crops are judged by a dual standard. They must deliver biomass, and they must deliver the right chemistry. These two objectives often trade off, and both depend critically on how plants acquire, allocate, and respond to nutrients. In field practice, this trade-off is poorly understood, and is too often masked by heavy fertilizer use, with environmental costs that contradict the very ethos of medicinal cultivation.

Building on our earlier work on nutrient signaling in Marchantia and stress responses in polyploid cotton, we dissect the molecular networks that couple nutrient status to secondary metabolism in medicinal crops. We integrate multi-omics profiling under controlled and field conditions with high-throughput phenotyping, with the goal of identifying the regulatory nodes where yield and quality can be jointly optimized.

We see this work as the molecular substrate for a more precise and greener model of medicinal-plant agriculture. The aim is cultivation strategies grounded in plant biology rather than in empirical fertilizer escalation, and varieties designed to be productive, high-quality, and resource-efficient at the same time.

Approaches

How we work

Methodologically, we are an omics and systems-driven lab. We treat plants as integrated biological systems, and we invest in building the technical platforms that let us interrogate medicinal plants at the same depth and rigor enjoyed by model species.

Multi-omics

Genome assembly and comparative genomics, bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and targeted metabolomics, used together to connect genotype, gene regulation, and chemistry.

Phenomics and systems biology

High-throughput phenotyping under controlled and field conditions, integrated with network-level modeling and machine-learning approaches to extract regulatory logic from large datasets.

Genetic transformation

A core technology focus of the lab is to establish robust Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, tissue culture, and CRISPR/Cas9 platforms for our target medicinal species, moving them from intractable to experimentally addressable, and laying the foundation for molecular-design breeding.
